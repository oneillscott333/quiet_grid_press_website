The 7 in Issue 4 belonged in row 4, and a tip of the cap to everyone who walked the column. This issue we finally talk about pencil marks themselves: when to write them, how many, and where the whole practice goes wrong.

## Three squares

**One.** Pencil marks are a tool, not a religion. Write candidates only where they help, and only two or three of them per square. A grid with every square crammed full of tiny numbers hides more than it shows; your eye cannot find the lonely candidate in a crowd of forty. Mark the squares that are nearly decided, leave the wide-open ones blank, and let the grid tell you where to look.

**Two.** The first cool morning of autumn, a warm mug, and a grid. Nobody planned it as a ritual. It became one anyway.

**Three.** Behind the grid: we have started writing down what we finish. Date, source, difficulty, and three honest words about how it went. It turns out a page of finished puzzles is its own kind of satisfaction, and it makes the next one better. There is a little book coming about exactly this, and a free page of it to print, right after the new year. More soon.

## One grid

Row 2 of this grid has three empty squares. Pencil in the candidates for each one, then place the square that has only one choice:

```
1  .  . | .  6  . | 9  .  .
7  a  3 | 4  9  5 | b  c  2
.  5  . | .  .  . | .  .  6
--------+---------+--------
.  .  . | .  .  1 | 6  2  .
4  .  . | .  5  . | .  3  9
.  9  2 | 7  .  . | .  .  .
--------+---------+--------
2  4  . | 5  .  . | 1  .  .
.  .  . | 2  8  . | .  .  .
8  7  . | 3  1  . | 2  .  .
```

Row 2 is missing 1, 6, and 8. For each of squares a, b, and c, look down its column and around its box and cross off what is already taken. One of the three will be left with a single candidate. Which square, and which digit?
