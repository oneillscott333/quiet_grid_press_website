## Three squares

**One.** A naked pair. Find two squares in the same row, column, or box that have exactly the same two candidates, say 6 and 8, and nothing else. Neither square knows which of the two it will be, but between them they have both digits spoken for. So no other square in that unit can be a 6 or an 8. Cross those candidates off elsewhere, and very often a third square drops to a single. Two squares, one promise, one free answer.

**Two.** Wrapping a book for someone who will actually use it. Not display it, not mean to get to it. Use it, pencil in hand, on a Tuesday in February. That is the best kind of present, and it is why we make ours the way we do.

## One grid

In row 6 of this grid, two squares share the same two candidates. Find the pair, then place the digit it frees in another square of that row:

```
2  .  6 | .  .  7 | .  4  .
.  .  . | .  5  9 | .  6  .
4  .  5 | .  .  . | .  1  .
--------+---------+--------
.  .  . | 7  .  . | .  3  .
3  .  9 | .  4  . | 6  .  .
1  a  7 | b  c  d | 5  2  4
--------+---------+--------
.  7  1 | .  2  3 | .  .  .
.  .  . | .  7  4 | .  9  1
5  3  . | 9  .  . | .  .  .
```

Row 6 is missing 3, 6, 8, and 9, with four empty squares: a (column 2), b (column 4), c (column 5), and d (column 6). Two of those four squares can each be only 6 or 8. Once you see which two, one of the remaining squares has just one digit left. Which square, and which digit?
